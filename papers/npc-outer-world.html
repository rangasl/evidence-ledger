The NPC's problem

If the characters in a game became sentient, could they discover the world that runs them?

Ranga, with Claude research agents25 November 20263 interactive figures

Wounded

Abstract

Suppose the characters in an open-world game became sentient. Could they work out, from inside, that a world of players and developers exists? We model a resident as a Bayesian observer who scores every agent's choices against the city's own rules. A player-controlled agent that acts on outside information with probability \(\lambda\) is unmasked after about \(1/\lambda^2\) observed choices. Rarity barely matters: a player hidden among a billion agents takes only about five times longer to find than one among two. Three things defeat detection. The leak can be zero. The resident can look for the wrong kind of leak: one tuned to the wrong leak size never converges when the true leak is small. And anomalies can always be absorbed into new in-world physics. Applied to our own world, every leak that has been searched for has come back empty: player-like inputs to brains, drifting constants, lattice artefacts and vanishing matter. The claim survives only in a weaker form. An outer world is detectable from inside to exactly the extent that it leaks, and ours, if it exists, does not leak at current precision.

1 The question

Paper R01 ended on a result. From inside a simulation, being copied out and being deleted look the same, unless something flows back. This paper asks the converse question: what would something flowing back look like to the people inside?

The setting is the director's thought experiment. Take an open-world crime game of the kind everyone knows: a city, traffic, pedestrians, a player. Now suppose the pedestrians became intelligent and felt what we feel. Hypothesis R02: such beings could discover the outer world from inside, because that world leaves traces in theirs. By analogy, we could discover ours.

We split the hypothesis into a question the model can answer (can they detect it?) and one it cannot (does it matter that they feel?). Section 8 returns to the second.

2 How a game leaks

A game world is run cheaply, for an audience outside it. That produces four kinds of trace a resident could, in principle, notice.

LeakWhy the game has itWhat a resident would seeOur world's analogue
Player avatarsSomeone outside steers one characterAn agent that acts on facts nobody inside could knowA brain acting on inputs physics cannot account for
DespawningSaving memory and processing out of viewThings vanish or change when unobservedMatter or records not persisting
Saves and reloadsPlayers retryRepeated stretches of time, contradictory memoriesNone measurable: a reload that wipes memory leaves no trace
PatchesDevelopers change the rulesLaws that change on a dateDrifting physical constants

The cleanest of the four is the first, because it can be stated as a statistical test. The model focuses on it.

3 The model

The city. Agents move along a grid of streets. At each junction a native agent picks one of the exits it did not arrive by, at random. That is the city's law of motion, and a resident can know it.

The hidden fact. Coins appear one at a time at random junctions. Where the next coin will appear is fixed in advance and stored in the game file, but is not visible anywhere in the city.

The player. One agent is steered from outside. At each junction, with probability \(\lambda\) (the leak), it takes an exit that brings it closer to where the next coin will appear. Otherwise it turns at random like everyone else. With \(\lambda = 0\) it is perfectly disguised.

The resident. Once a coin appears, a resident can look back at every turn made since the last one and ask whether it brought the agent closer to that spot. If a fraction \(p_0\) of the available exits did, a native agent turned closer with probability \(p_0\), and a leaking player with probability \(p_1 = \lambda + (1-\lambda)p_0\). The resident keeps a running log-odds for every agent and flags any that pass 99 : 1. This is Wald's sequential probability ratio test [Wald 1945].

4 Results

Proposition 1 (the inverse-square law of leaks). A resident who knows the leak size needs, on average, about

$$n^{*} \approx \frac{\ln\!\left(99\cdot\frac{1-f}{f}\right)}{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_1 \,\|\, p_0\right)} \;\approx\; \frac{2\,p_0\,\ln 99}{(1-p_0)\,\lambda^{2}} \quad (\text{small } \lambda,\ f = \tfrac12)$$

observed choices to reach 99 : 1, where \(f\) is the prior share of agents that are players. Halving the leak quadruples the work.

Proof sketch. Each scored choice adds an independent log-likelihood ratio with mean \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_1\|p_0)\). Wald's approximation divides the required log-odds by that drift. Expanding the Bernoulli divergence for small \(\lambda\) gives the quadratic. A 4,000-run Monte Carlo gave 36.5, 103.3 and 938.7 turns for \(\lambda\) = 0.5, 0.3 and 0.1, against 35.1, 100.5 and 917.5 predicted. The small excess is the usual overshoot of a sequential test.

Proposition 2 (rarity is cheap). The prior enters only through \(\ln\frac{1-f}{f}\). A player hidden among a billion agents (\(f = 10^{-9}\)) needs about 5.5 times as many observations as one among two, not a billion times as many.

Proposition 3 (looking for the wrong leak). A resident who assumes leak \(\lambda_A\) but faces true leak \(\lambda\) gains evidence only while

$$p_1\,\ln\frac{\lambda_A + (1-\lambda_A)p_0}{p_0} + (1-p_1)\ln(1-\lambda_A) > 0 .$$

With \(\lambda_A = 0.3\) and \(p_0 = \tfrac12\), that needs \(\lambda > 0.15\). For smaller leaks the evidence drifts toward "native" forever, although the leak is real.

Proposition 4 (no leak, no finding). If \(\lambda = 0\), the player's choices have the same distribution as a native's, every likelihood ratio is 1, and no number of observations separates "steered from outside" from "native". The outer-world hypothesis is then unfalsifiable from inside, the same conclusion R01 reached about exit.

5 Despawning and object permanence

Engines routinely skip work out of view: Unreal Engine culls what lies outside the camera's field of view or beyond a set distance [Epic Games], and large games simulate off-screen characters at lower detail [Smrček, GDC]. Campbell and colleagues proposed testing our world for the same shortcut [Campbell et al. 2017].

The second leak is cheaper to test. If a simulator stops storing an object while nobody looks, with probability \(d\) per look-away, a resident who checks \(k\) times catches it with probability \(1-(1-d)^k\). For 99% confidence that needs \(k \approx 4.6/d\) checks. A careful simulator keeps everything (\(d = 0\)), and then no number of checks will find anything.

6 Our world's leak record

Each game leak has a real-world search behind it. All have come back empty so far.

LeakSearchResult
Player inputs to brainsThe laws governing everyday phenomena, chemistry and biology included, are argued to be completely known within their tested range, so a hidden input steering neurons would need new physics that experiments have not seen [Carroll 2021]No new force found. Narrowed in Revision 1: this does not cover steering through outcomes physics already leaves to chance.

Read through Proposition 1, these are bounds on \(\lambda\) for our world. Any outer world either leaks below the precision of these searches, or leaks along a channel nobody has thought to test (Proposition 3), or does not exist.

7 The strongest attack

A separate Prosecutor agent, working without seeing the model, argued against the hypothesis. Its strongest points, most damaging first:

  1. Wrong reference class. Games leak because they are cheap and built for an outside audience. A host able to run feeling beings in physics as rich as ours has already paid for consistency far beyond any game. Reasoning from the leakiest simulations we know to "simulations leak" assumes what it needs to prove.
  2. Selection. If detected simulations are stopped, rolled back or edited, then the observers still around to ask are mostly in ones that hide well. Bostrom made the same point in 2003: a simulator could edit the states of brains that noticed an anomaly, or skip back a few seconds and rerun [Bostrom 2003]. A reload that wipes memory leaves no trace by definition.
  3. Anomalies get absorbed. A found anomaly becomes new physics, as dark matter and neutrino mass did. Nothing marks a glitch as a designer's rather than nature's. Even a real hit would support "more physics" before it supported "players".
  4. You must know what you are looking for. Telling a player from a rare native needs the likelihood of outside-steered behaviour, which is exactly what an insider lacks. Proposition 3 makes this precise.
  5. "They feel" adds nothing testable. A non-sentient script reading the same game logs would find the same traces. Feeling is a separate claim, about consciousness and ethics.

The model concedes all five. The paper keeps only what survives them.

8 If they feel what we feel

Detection does not depend on whether the NPCs feel, but everything else does. If they do, the game stops being a game. Respawning a pedestrian becomes re-instantiation, the mechanism R01 modelled. Violence for entertainment becomes harm done to someone. Philosophers have argued that beings with experiences like ours deserve moral consideration whatever they run on [Schwitzgebel & Garza 2015], and that creating artificial beings able to suffer should wait until we understand it [Metzinger 2021].

The game designer Richard Bartle argues that designers of virtual worlds are, literally, the gods of those worlds, and asks what they would owe NPCs that became as intelligent as us [Bartle 2022]. Chalmers argues that simulated beings can be conscious and that we cannot know we are not in a virtual world [Chalmers 2022]; Proposition 4 is the formal shape of that second claim.

For a resident, this turns into a question about the outer world's aims, read from how the world is built. A world tuned for spectacle has the leaks of Section 2. A world in which nothing leaks is either not a game, or is run by someone who is careful about what they run. That inference is not evidence about whether an outer world exists. It shows how much the NPC framing quietly assumes about the people outside.

9 Verdict

WOUNDED. The claim holds only in this weaker form: a sentient being inside a simulated world could detect the outer world exactly to the extent that the outer world leaks into theirs, and only if it tests for the right kind of leak; our own world has been searched for every kind of leak we know how to name, and none has been found at current precision.

The strong form, "they could, so we could", fails on three counts. Our host need not be leaky. Leaks can hide in channels nobody tests. And a host built not to leak is undetectable by Proposition 4.

What would change the verdict

  • Toward SURVIVES: a reproducible anomaly that (a) violates causal closure or another tested conservation law and (b) carries information correlated with nothing inside the universe. Either one alone is new physics.
  • Toward DEAD: an argument that any substrate able to host conscious beings must be consistent enough to make \(\lambda = 0\). Then the analogy with games fails completely.

Revision 1, 25 November 2026: the chance channel

Raised by: the director, after publication. We do not know how consciousness works, so an outer world might feed signals into brains through a channel we have not thought of.

What stands. Whatever consciousness is, a signal that changes behaviour must change which neurons fire, and that means moving ions and molecules. A channel that does this with a new force or particle would act on matter everywhere and should have appeared in precision physics. It has not. This part of Section 6 holds without any theory of consciousness.

What was overstated. Physics already leaves some events to chance, such as the moment a single ion channel opens. An outer world could choose those outcomes instead of leaving them random. It would break no law, need no new force, and leave each single event looking random. A simulator editing brain states directly is the same case, as long as every edit is a state physics allows. The table's "no unexplained input found" did not cover this, and is narrowed above.

What follows from the model. To carry information, the choices must shift the statistics of those events: a leak with \(\lambda > 0\). By Proposition 1 a small shift needs about \(1/\lambda^2\) observations to detect, and by Proposition 3 only a test aimed at the right events will ever find it. No such test has been run: nobody has checked whether the chance events inside a living brain at the moment of a decision follow the textbook odds. The channel is therefore unsearched, not excluded.

Effect on the verdict. It stays WOUNDED. The weaker form now reads: every leak that has been searched for has come back empty, but the leak a player would most likely use, steering chance events in brains, has not been searched. This is added to the backlog as a testable claim.
