# Day 1 — Does the brain decide before you do? (Libet 1983)

**Track:** FW · **Claim ID:** FW01 · **Date:** 2026-11-24
**Verdict:** WOUNDED — the timing holds; reading it as "no free will" does not.

## The finding
Libet, Gleason, Wright & Pearl (1983) recorded scalp EEG from 5 subjects (≥ 6 sessions each)
while they flicked their wrist whenever they felt like it. Each EEG trace is an average of 40
movements, aligned backwards from the moment the muscle fired. Subjects watched a spot circling
a clock and afterwards reported where it was when they first felt the urge to move ("W").
For fully spontaneous movements, a slow negative shift in the EEG — the readiness potential
(RP) — began on average **~350 ms before W** (never less than ~150 ms). When subjects had
some pre-planning, the gap grew to ~800 ms.

## What it seems to show
The reported urge to move is not the first measurable event. Something in the brain is already
changing before the person says they decided. The ordering has been seen again with a method
that does not rely on memory: real-time tone probes (Matsuhashi & Hallett 2008). Single-neuron
recordings in 12 patients found supplementary motor area cells that predicted the movement with
more than 80% accuracy ~700 ms before reported awareness (Fried, Mukamel & Kreiman 2011).

## The strongest attack
**The RP may not be a decision at all.**
- Schurger, Sitt & Dehaene (2012) model the brain as a noisy accumulator: when there is no
  reason to move, random background fluctuations decide when a threshold is crossed. Averaging
  EEG backwards from the movement then produces a slow ramp anyway. In their model the actual
  "decision" (threshold crossing) lands ~150 ms before movement — the same time people report
  the urge. A new prediction of the model was confirmed: people who were interrupted responded
  faster when that negativity was already higher.
- The RP is no larger before deciding *to* move than before deciding *not* to (Trevena & Miller 2010).
- People can still cancel a movement after the RP has started, up to ~200 ms before it
  (Schultze-Kraft et al. 2016).

**The urge time "W" is not a timestamp.** Delaying feedback about the movement pushes reported
W later — a 120 ms video delay moved it ~44 ms (Banks & Isham 2009). People reconstruct when
they decided from when they seem to have acted. A real-time probe puts intention ~1 s earlier
than Libet's W, which shrinks the very gap the claim rests on (Matsuhashi & Hallett 2008).

**It only covers meaningless choices.** When the same 18 people made choices with real
consequences (where to send $1000 donations), the RP was "absent — or at least substantially
diminished" (Maoz, Yaffe, Koch & Mudrik 2019). One study, but it is the only direct test.

**Correction to the usual "best support":** Haggard & Eimer (1999) found the general RP did not
track the timing of awareness — it tended to come *later* when awareness came earlier. Their
lateralised RP result did not replicate (Schlegel et al. 2013).

## Judgement
**WOUNDED.** What survives, exactly:
> For arbitrary, purposeless, self-paced movements, averaged EEG shows a slow negativity
> beginning several hundred ms before the reported urge. So the reported urge is not the
> first measurable event before such movements.

What does not survive: that the brain *decided* unconsciously, or that this applies to the
choices free will is actually about. And even a perfect result here only argues against a
version of free will that needs an uncaused conscious trigger — a premise this experiment
does not test.

**Would revive it (→ SURVIVES):** a preregistered study that predicts *whether and when* a
movement happens from single-trial brain activity > 500 ms before the urge, analysed forward
in time (not back-averaged), with a non-retrospective W, and that holds for deliberate choices.
**Would kill it (→ DEAD):** a forward-analysis replication where activity > 300 ms before W
predicts no better than chance, or where the RP-before-W ordering disappears under a bias-free
measure of W.
