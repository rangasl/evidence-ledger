# Day 5 — Can electric current to the brain make you want to move? (Desmurget 2009)

**Track:** FW · **Claim ID:** FW04 · **Date:** 2026-11-28
**Verdict:** DEAD — parietal stimulation does produce a reported wish to move, but that shows how the feeling is made, not that free will is absent. Every view of free will predicts it.

## The finding
Desmurget, Reilly, Richard, Szathmari, Mottolese & Sirigu (2009) applied direct electrical stimulation
to the cortex of 7 awake patients during brain-tumour surgery. Only some patients had parietal sites that
produced the effect. We did not read the full text, so we give no per-patient count. Evidence was the
patients' spoken reports, and the stimulation was not blinded. The abstract reports three results.
**Right inferior parietal cortex:** a "strong intention and desire to move" the opposite hand, arm or foot.
**Left inferior parietal cortex:** an intention to move the lips and to talk. **Higher current at the same
parietal sites:** patients believed they had actually made the movement, although EMG showed no muscle
activity. **Premotor stimulation:** real mouth and limb movements, which patients "firmly denied" making.
Press coverage quotes patients using words like "will", "desire" and "wanting to" without prompting
("I felt a desire to lick my lips"). We have not checked that wording against the full text.

## What it seems to show
The feeling of willing and the act itself come apart in both directions. One site gives wanting, and
then "I did it", with no movement. Another gives movement with no sense of having done it. An outside
current can switch the feeling on. So the report "I chose to do that" can appear when the real cause
was a stimulator. That undercuts the argument for free will from experience ("I feel that I choose"):
introspection cannot certify that a choice caused an action. This fits views like Wegner's, in which
felt will is the brain's estimate of what it is doing.

## The strongest attack
**A felt desire is not an act of will.** No induced urge was weighed, chosen between, or acted on.
The experiment produced one kind of experience, the feeling of wanting to move. That is no more
surprising than current evoking tingling or a flash of light. Evoked visual hallucinations do not show
that sight is unreal. The free-will question is whether agents control their decisions, not whether the
feelings that come with decisions are physical.

**Every position predicts the result.** Compatibilists, and libertarians who accept physicalism,
already identify intentions with brain states. Even a dualist expects current in cortex to change
experience. No one predicted that parietal stimulation would *fail* to evoke a feeling of wanting.

**The evidence is thin.** Seven patients, a subset responding, unblinded verbal report. We found no
independent sham-controlled replication. The main follow-ups are reviews by the same group (Desmurget &
Sirigu 2009, 2012). Karnath, Borchers & Himmelbach (2010) argued that direct stimulation may switch cortex
*off* rather than on, which weakens the authors' "increased parietal activity" explanation. It does not
change the fact that the feeling was evoked.

**The feeling is not unique to parietal cortex.** Fried et al. (1991) evoked an "urge" to move from the
supplementary motor area "in some cases". Related lesion work (Sirigu et al. 2004) is about *timing*:
parietal patients could not report *when* they first became aware of intending to move. It does not show
that they lost the feeling of intending.

## Judgement
**DEAD.** What was physically measured: unblinded spoken reports from awake patients while current was
applied to the cortex. The first half of the claim holds: stimulating parietal cortex creates a reported
felt intention to move. The second half ("'will' can be produced artificially") treats the *experience*
of intending as if it were *will*. Nothing was chosen or done.

The Advocate's best version is real: the experience of willing is a brain output, so introspection
cannot prove that a choice caused an action. That removes one argument *for* free will. It is not
evidence *against* free will, because the result is equally expected whether free will exists or not.

Surviving weaker form: "External current to the inferior parietal cortex can evoke a reported feeling of
wanting to move, and of having moved, with no movement."
**Weaker form is evidence for the thesis:** no, because it is equally expected with or without free will.
It undercuts the argument from felt choice but says nothing about whether agents control their decisions.

**Would revive it:** stimulation that biases a real choice between options, which the person then claims
as freely chosen and acts on, replicated with a sham control. Brasil-Neto et al. (1992) is a partial
example: magnetic stimulation over motor cortex biased which hand people chose to move, and they did not notice the bias. That is a separate claim for the
backlog, not FW04.
