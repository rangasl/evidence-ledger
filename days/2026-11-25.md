# Day 2 — If one of three things must be true, are we simulated? (Bostrom 2003)

**Track:** SIM · **Claim ID:** SIM01 · **Date:** 2026-11-25
**Verdict:** WOUNDED — the trilemma is valid logic, but it puts the odds of simulation near a third to a half, not "almost certainly".

## The finding
Not an experiment: a counting argument. Bostrom (2003) defines the fraction of all observers with
human-type experiences who are simulated as f_sim = f_P·f_I·N_I / (f_P·f_I·N_I + 1), where f_P is the
fraction of civilisations that reach a posthuman stage, f_I the fraction of those interested in
ancestor simulations, and N_I how many each runs. He estimates a human brain at ~10^14–10^17 ops/s,
so all human mental history needs ~10^33–10^36 operations. A planetary-mass computer (a figure he
credits to another author) could do ~10^42 ops/s, so one ancestor simulation would take less than
a millionth of its capacity for one second. N_I would then be huge, so at least one holds:
(1) f_P ≈ 0, (2) f_I ≈ 0, or (3) f_sim ≈ 1. An extra "bland indifference" principle turns (3)
into "you are almost certainly simulated."

## What it seems to show
Given substrate-independent consciousness, the trilemma is close to forced by arithmetic, and the
compute margin is wide enough that errors of several orders of magnitude don't change it. You
cannot consistently believe all three of: we will reach technological maturity, mature
civilisations run many ancestor simulations, and we are not simulated. Bostrom & Kulczycki (2011)
patched a real bug: one long-lived civilisation could outnumber everyone else's observers.

## The strongest attack
**Proving "one of three" is not evidence for the third.** The trilemma holds just as well in base
reality, and horns (1) or (2) can carry all the weight. Bostrom himself splits his credence
"roughly evenly" between the three. Kipping (2020) redoes it as a Bayesian model comparison. With
even prior odds between physical and simulated, the chance we are simulated stays just under 50%
and tends towards 50% as simulations multiply, with physical reality slightly favoured. He notes
the media has described the idea "not just a possibility but in fact a high probability". His
own result does not support that.
- **It undermines itself (Birch 2013).** The argument trusts our physics about future computing
  limits. Horn (3) says that physics comes from a simulator and need not match base reality.
  Leaning on (3) removes the premises that were meant to establish it.
- **The step to "therefore me" is contested (Weatherson 2003; Bostrom's reply 2005).** Going from
  "most observers are simulated" to "I am probably simulated" relies on an indifference principle.
  Weatherson argues no reading of it is both plausible and strong enough. That attack targets only
  this last step, not the trilemma.

## Judgement
**WOUNDED.** What survives, exactly:
> Given substrate-independent consciousness and Bostrom's indifference principle, one cannot
> coherently hold all three of: we will reach posthumanity, posthumans run many ancestor
> simulations, and we are not simulated. The argument raises credence in simulation only as far
> as one's credence that (1) and (2) are false. Unconditionally it gives about 1/3 (Bostrom) or
> just under 1/2 (Kipping, even priors), not "almost certainly". It says nothing about free will.

What does not survive: "we are almost certainly living in a simulation." On its own, the bare
disjunction cannot be checked from inside, so it also gets a mark in the unfalsifiable tally.
It is still not fully unfalsifiable: Kipping identifies one future observation that would move
the number sharply (below).

**Would revive it (→ SURVIVES):** we ourselves start running conscious ancestor-type simulations.
In Kipping's model, the chance that we are the base reality then falls towards zero. Strong
independent evidence against horns (1) and (2) would also do it.
**Would kill it (→ DEAD):** evidence that consciousness is not substrate-independent (simulated
people would not be observers, so the count collapses), or Birch's selective-scepticism objection
shown to be decisive.
