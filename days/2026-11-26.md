# Day 3 — Can a brain scan read your choice seconds before you make it? (Soon 2008)

**Track:** FW · **Claim ID:** FW02 · **Date:** 2026-11-26
**Verdict:** WOUNDED — there is a small, early lean toward one button; "can predict a choice" overstates it.

## The finding
Soon, Brass, Heinze & Haynes (2008) screened 36 people and scanned the 14 whose spontaneous
left/right choices were balanced; 12 were analysed. In a 3T scanner (one image every 2 s), subjects
watched letters change every 500 ms, pressed a left or right button whenever they felt like it,
and afterwards reported which letter was on screen when they consciously decided. A pattern
classifier read fine-grained activity in frontopolar cortex and precuneus and predicted the
button at **~55–60% accuracy (chance 50%)**. The earliest signal came **~7 s before** the
reported decision; allowing for the slow blood-flow (BOLD) lag, the neural activity is inferred
to start up to **~10 s** before. Motor areas carried the choice only around the press itself.

## What it seems to show
Day 1 (Libet) found a gap of a few hundred milliseconds. This pushes it to seconds, and moves it
out of motor cortex into high-level control regions. Overall activity did not differ between
left and right choices; the information was in the spatial pattern. Areas that predicted *when*
to press (pre-SMA/SMA) differed from those that predicted *which* button. The same lab repeated
the task at 7T (Bode et al. 2011, N = 11): decoding was significant from ~7.5 s before the
decision, with a peak of 57% just before it (−1.5 s). The lab later extended the result to an
abstract add-vs-subtract choice, decodable 4 s before the reported decision (Soon et al. 2013).
All of this comes from one lab. There is no independent replication.

## The strongest attack
**History alone predicts as well as the brain scan.** People asked to choose at random are not
random. Lages & Jaworska (2012) ran the task without a scanner. A classifier using only each
person's previous responses reached 61.6% (study 1) and 64.1% (study 2). Two limits on that
attack: these were different subjects from Soon 2008, and the 61.6% applies only to the 12 who
passed the balance criterion. With all subjects included, study 1 drops to 55.4% (p = 0.105, not
significant). The Haynes lab accepts the core point. Its own reanalysis puts prediction of trial
N from earlier trials at ~62–64% (a classifier that also used base-rate differences), and calls
the original randomness check "only an approximate assessment" (Soon et al. 2014, citing
Allefeld et al. 2013).

**Selection and a fixed chance level.** 24 of 36 people screened were not analysed, mostly for
unbalanced choices, and trials were rebalanced. Lages, Boyle & Jaworska (2013) argue that
balancing base rates still leaves imbalances in left-to-right transitions that a searchlight
classifier can exploit. They recommend permutation baselines in place of a fixed 50% chance level,
and analyses that condition on the previous response.

**The rebuttal, stated fairly.** Soon et al. (2014) refit the decoder with labels shifted by one
trial and found no information about the previous choice in any region. Accuracy also rose with
distance from the previous trial. That weakens the "it's just leftover history" story. It does
not address subject selection, and it does not show brain decoding beating history decoding in
the same subjects.

**What was physically measured.** A ~10-point lean in a binary, meaningless choice, timed against
a "moment of decision" that subjects recalled afterwards by remembering a letter, using a signal
sampled every 2 s.

## Judgement
**WOUNDED.** What survives, exactly:
> In 12 pre-selected subjects, fMRI activity patterns in frontopolar cortex and precuneus
> predicted a binary left/right button choice at about 55–60% accuracy (chance is 50%). The
> earliest signal came about 7 s before reported awareness; allowing for BOLD lag, the neural
> activity is inferred to start up to about 10 s before. The only replication is from the same
> lab (Bode 2011: 11 subjects, peak 57%). This accuracy is no higher than what a person's
> previous choices alone predict (about 62–64% in the lab's own data). A label-shift control
> suggests the signal is not simply left over from the previous trial.

What does not survive: that fMRI "can predict" the choice in any strong sense. A ~60% lean, which
history also explains, does not establish where a choice originates. The decisive analysis has
not been run: does brain decoding beat history decoding in the same subjects, against a
permutation baseline?

**Would revive it (→ SURVIVES):** an independent, preregistered replication with no subject
pre-selection and permutation baselines, where decoding stays significant after conditioning on
the previous choice, or beats a history model in the same subjects.
**Would kill it (→ DEAD):** an independent replication that fails, or decoding that disappears
once trial history is conditioned on or a permutation baseline replaces the fixed 50% chance level.
